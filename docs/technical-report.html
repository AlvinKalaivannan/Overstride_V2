Making a Null Trustworthy

We set out to measure what a single camera costs an injury-screening model. The answer was “almost nothing, because there was almost nothing there.” This is how that null was made worth believing.

A negative result is only as good as the evidence that it is not incompetence. This report is about the second part: the sequence of checks that turned “we found nothing” into “nothing is there, and here is the smallest thing we could have found.”

The measurement itself takes one paragraph. Injury screening for runners is normally done in a motion-capture lab; we asked how much classification performance is lost when the same lower-limb kinematics come from one camera instead. On the only task in the data that carries kinematic injury signal, monocular video costs 0.027–0.033 AUC. That is small — and it is small because the signal it degrades is itself small.

The rest is method, and the method is the part worth copying.

The data

The Ferber / Running Injury Clinic dataset: 1,798 runners, 2,506 treadmill sessions, marker-based motion capture. Every label describes injury status at the time of testing, so nothing here forecasts future injury, and no per-runner verdict is produced anywhere in this work.

Five moves

Each became possible only because the previous one failed. The order is not presentational — it is the order the evidence forced.

1

Declare the bar before looking

0 of 60 pre-registered tests survived correction — the kill criterion fired

Sixty tests were specified in advance, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction and a written kill criterion: if kinematics could not beat a demographics-only control, the gait data carries no injury signal and no downstream video pipeline rescues it.

It fired. Every subsequent claim in this project is made against a bar that had already been missed once, which is a very different posture from searching until something clears.

2

Find out what the model is actually using

Paperwork beats physiology — 0.775 against 0.700

Rather than accept the failure, we asked what the scores that did look promising were made of. A model built only from questionnaire missingness and collection year — no physiology whatsoever — outperformed the demographics control.

Questionnaire completeness tracks the study wave, and the waves differ in injury mix. Collection wave is recoverable from session file structure at 0.635 accuracy against a 0.336 baseline. Most of what looked like a gait signal was an artefact of how the data was collected.

This is the finding we would most want another team to check for in their own data, and it cost nothing to look for.

3

Redesign so the confound cannot operate

0.610 [0.532, 0.686], with every confound constant by construction

Instead of controlling for provenance statistically, we changed the unit of comparison: a runner’s injured limb against their own healthy limb, in the same trial. Demographics, provenance, file structure and limb dominance are then identical on both sides of the comparison — not adjusted for, but arithmetically unable to contribute.

All four were re-measured on this design and all four sit at chance. The task clears its pre-registered bar at 0.610 against a 0.517 base rate.

It then fails every deployment requirement, which is why no tool was built on it: PPV 0.574 against that 0.517 base rate, a calibration slope of 0.716, and agreement of only 0.667 on repeat scans of the same runner. Averaging a runner’s sessions does not help (0.587 → 0.583), so the error is subject-specific rather than random — the model is consistently wrong about the same people.

4

Prove the method can find what it claims to miss

The design resolves 0.25° — the ceiling is not an underpowered test

This is the move that makes the null worth anything, and the one most often skipped.

Every control so far was negative: provenance, demographics, structure and limb dominance all at chance, establishing that the design does not leak. Nothing established the converse — that the method has the power to find an asymmetry that is genuinely present. Without that, “we found nothing” and “this method finds nothing” are not separated by the evidence.

So we injected a limb asymmetry of known magnitude into the real waveforms, on the real folds, through the exact model that produced 0.610, and swept the magnitude.

The design resolves a consistent asymmetry of 0.25° at AUC 0.681, and 0.50° at 0.844. That is far below anything a clinician would call an asymmetry, and inside the noise of most marker labs. A design with that much power did not miss a real effect.

Read backwards, the sweep converts the result into a physical quantity: the observed 0.610 carries as much discriminative information as ≈0.17° RMS of consistent asymmetry, against a between-subject spread of 2.86°. Two very different injection shapes agreed on that figure within 10%, which is why it is quotable — it is a property of the design, not of what we chose to inject.

The limit of this claim

The injection is consistent across subjects, which is the easiest possible signal and almost certainly not how real injury presents. So the correct statement is “0.17° of consistent asymmetry”, not “the real asymmetry is 0.17°.” A heterogeneous effect of larger magnitude could produce the same result.

Two are worth singling out. The viewpoint result replaced an extrapolated +0.07° penalty with a measurement across a genuinely wide range of camera geometries: accuracy improves monotonically as the view becomes side-on, which is the geometry the application prescribes. And the 1.84× degradation is a real qualification we did not want — the lifting path is measurably dataset-sensitive, with no overlap between the two subject groups.

The corrections ledger

Eighteen published claims moved during this work. Keeping the ledger is the single practice we would most recommend, for a reason that only becomes visible once it exists: almost none of these were caught by a test failing. They were caught by chasing a number that looked wrong.

What was believedWhat is trueHow it surfaced
Joint positions in millimetres Pixels, with a per-frame conversion factor — MPJPE 11.8 mm became 51.7 mm An 11.8 mm error would have beaten every published result. Checked against anatomy: a femur came out at 493 mm divided, an absurd 36 mm multiplied.
Three injury conditions significant Zero survive A fold-level test returned q = 0.000 for one condition whose own confidence interval ran [0.410, 0.986]. Two numbers that cannot both describe the same data.
Accuracy rises as framerate falls An artefact; the trend is monotone downward The result was physically implausible, so the pipeline was re-read. Noise was being added at full resolution and then low-passed away by the decimation itself.
A cleaner cohort explains a weaker result Withdrawn — the comparison does not hold The “worst leakage” figure was below chance, and leakage pushes controls above it. It was noise being read as contamination.
A coordinate frame from the documentation The data was never in that frame A wrong rotation still yields smooth, physiological-looking curves. Its only symptom was a treadmill speed of 1.85 m/s on a 2.5 m/s trial.
A joint disagreed badly in magnitude A sign convention differed; magnitudes agreed to 0.92° The metric reported near-perfect shape agreement beside a 28° error — impossible for one pair of curves. A pure sign flip makes that error twice the signal amplitude.

Three of the six above changed a conclusion, and every one of those made the work more defensible rather than less: a gap closed, a set of false positives removed, an implausible artefact eliminated.

What is still open

Stated with reasons, because a limitations section that only lists things you could not have fixed is worth less than one that says which door is closed and why.

The tool has never run on real footage blocked

The video pipeline has been exercised on synthetic clips and on released keypoints, never on video. The one accessible dataset pairing running video with 3D ground truth serves its archive through a public file share that refused the video across five attempts and several days — while serving a 1.46 GB sibling file without difficulty. The blocker is distribution, not method.

One clinic, one protocol no fix available

The injury labels come from a single clinic. A dataset search established that no open source supplies a second cohort of clinically diagnosed runners with per-limb waveforms. The walking test does not close this — same clinic, same collection waves, same paperwork confound, largely the same people. A real fix is a collaboration, not a download.

A third limitation is structural rather than practical: the tool emits continuous traces while the analysis consumes stance-normalised curves, and nothing bridges them. There is no path from a video to the analysis the research built — deliberately, because the verdict at the end of that path is the one the deployment numbers showed to be unsupportable.

What we would take to the next project

  • A positive control is not optional for a null. It is the difference between “nothing was found” and “nothing was found, and here is the smallest thing this design could have found.” It cost hours and it is the section a reviewer would otherwise demand.
  • Ask what the good scores are made of, not just whether they are good. The provenance model took an afternoon and reframed the entire project.
  • Prefer designs where the confound is arithmetically impossible over designs where it is adjusted for. Within-subject comparison did what no amount of covariate control would have.
  • Write down what you got wrong, with the cause. The ledger is the most transferable artefact here — and the failure modes repeat across projects far more than the findings do.
  • Chase the number that looks wrong. Nearly every correction above came from that, and not one came from a test going red.
